# Fix the decoy shift before chasing better matchers

For our system, the biggest gain available right now does not come from a newer matching model. It comes from **correcting for decoy records that are denser on test than on train, slice by slice**, and then retraining on training data that has been made to look like test. The published state of the art for large-scale business entity resolution is well settled. Its main parts are several candidate generators whose outputs are pooled, a cheap filter model, a gradient-boosted matcher over string-similarity features with a fine-tuned transformer "cross-encoder" score fed in as an extra feature, graph-based post-processing, and precision-first decision rules. Our pipeline already has most of these parts. Against that backdrop, today's measurement stands out. On test the model accepts **0.1–0.18 more IDs per S1 entity than on train**, and the extra acceptances pile up in two slices: "near-twin" candidates (same name and street, different house number), which are **1.44 per S1 on US test vs 0.81 on train**, and French candidates with the same house number but a different name, which are accepted **0.61 times per S1 vs 0.36 on train**. Simple arithmetic puts the cost of one false extra ID at about 0.2 F0.5 for a typical S1 and 1.0 for an S1 that has no true match. So these extra acceptances are large enough, on their own, to explain the 0.0125 drop from our cross-validation score (0.978) to the public leaderboard (0.9655). The label-shift literature explains why the global prior correction we already ran (Saerens EM) could not see this problem. It also says what to do instead: estimate and correct the prior per slice, check the correction on validation folds deliberately shifted to look like test, and retrain with injected test-like decoys. After that come the techniques that raise the ceiling: a stacked multilingual cross-encoder for the 40% of missed matches that have empty addresses, collective features that compare the groups of candidates inside one S1's pool, and French-specific normalisation. The top team's 0.985 is above even our cross-validation score, so fixing the shift alone will not close the whole gap. Some of those ceiling-raising steps are needed too.

## Test decoys, not weak matching, explain most of the 0.0125 gap

Some definitions first, since every later step depends on them. S1 is the clean reference list of businesses. A **candidate pair** is one S1 entity together with one S2/S3 record that blocking retrieved for it. The stage-1 and stage-2 LightGBM models, after isotonic calibration, output P(match) for each pair. The decoder then picks, for each S1, the set of records with the highest *expected* F0.5. Our cross-validation (CV) score of 0.978 is the macro F0.5 of **out-of-fold (OOF)** predictions: the 551k training S1s are split into 5 groups with GroupKFold (all candidates of one S1 stay in the same group), and each group is scored by a model trained on the other four. A **near-twin** is a candidate with the S1's name and street but a different house number. Some near-twins are true matches carrying house-number noise; others are synthetic decoys.

The new measurement gives a concrete worked example. On US training data the model accepts 0.146 near-twins per S1 out of 0.81 near-twin candidates, which is **18% of near-twin candidates**. On US test there are 1.44 near-twin candidates per S1 and it accepts 0.199, which is 13.8%. So the model does lower its acceptance rate per candidate, but not by enough. Suppose the number of *true* near-twin matches per S1 is the same on test as on train (roughly 0.146, using train acceptances as a stand-in for train truth because the model is well calibrated per slice on train). Then only about 10% of test near-twin candidates are real, and the model over-accepts about **0.05 near-twins per S1 in the US**. In India near-twin candidates rise from 0.50 to 0.83 per S1. In France, "same house number, different name" acceptances rise by 0.25 per S1. Now the cost. An S1 with three true matches, all found, plus one false extra has precision 0.75 and recall 1, so F0.5 = 1.25·0.75/(0.25·0.75+1) = **0.79, a loss of 0.21**. An S1 with no true match that receives any ID scores 0 instead of 1. If the observed 0.1–0.18 extra IDs per S1 were all false, the macro loss would be roughly 0.02–0.036. The measured gap is 0.0125, which fits a picture in which somewhere between a third and two thirds of the extras are false. This is arithmetic, not a measurement. The pending leaderboard probe with France blanked is the first hard check on it.

The global Saerens EM check missed this for reasons the literature spells out. EM/MLLS (maximum-likelihood label-shift estimation) re-estimates the test match rate from unlabelled test probabilities. It is the strongest general-purpose estimator, beating BBSE and RLLS with about **8× lower weight-estimation error** when paired with bias-corrected calibration ([Alexandari et al., ICML 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06852)). But it assumes that P(features | label) is unchanged, and it is only consistent when the model is calibrated on the *target* data ([Garg et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07554)). Our decoys break both assumptions. First, the near-twin slice is a small share of all candidate pairs, so halving its prior barely moves the global rate. Second, inside the slice the new decoys are "hard": they look like the house-number-noise true matches seen in training, so P(features | non-match) has itself moved toward the match region. Where the model cannot tell two groups apart, EM has no signal with which to separate their priors. Product-matching benchmarks show the same pattern. On WDC Products, raising the share of look-alike non-matches ("corner cases") "negatively impacts the precision of matchers more than it does recall" for every model tested, Magellan-style feature models and transformers alike ([Peeters, Der & Bizer, EDBT 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09521)). Our 0.978 CV was measured at the training decoy mix, and the leaderboard is scored at the test mix.

## Ranks 1–3: correct the decoy shift where it lives

**Rank 1: a per-slice prior correction at decode time.** This is the cheapest test of the diagnosis, and it runs in CPU minutes. First split candidate pairs into slices: country × pair type, where the pair types are near-twin house number, same house number but different name, empty address (name only), and everything else. Then estimate a separate test prior in each slice. The two-class Saerens update is short: rescale each pair's odds by (π/π_s)/((1−π)/(1−π_s)), then set π to the mean of the rescaled probabilities, and repeat until it stops changing ([Saerens et al., Neural Computation 2002](https://direct.mit.edu/neco/article/14/1/21/6577/Adjusting-the-Outputs-of-a-Classifier-to-New-a)). Set π_s to the mean calibrated OOF probability in that slice, as Alexandari et al. recommend ([ICML 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06852)). As a cross-check that does not rely on calibration, use the two-class form of BBSE (Black Box Shift Estimation): π_t = (share of test pairs predicted positive − FPR)/(TPR − FPR), with TPR and FPR measured on OOF pairs ([Lipton et al., ICML 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916)). Because hard decoys can leave EM blind, add a third estimator that anchors on counts: pick the per-slice logit offset that brings test acceptances per S1 back to the training rate. This is the underlying assumption stated openly, namely that the organisers added decoys but not true matches. The same move, forcing the predicted group-size distribution to match train's, was **"the largest single trick"** of the Shopee 18th-place solution ([Shopee tricks summary](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/329472)). Worked on the US numbers above, the near-twin odds fall from 0.22 to 0.11, a logit offset of about **−0.67**. A lone near-twin candidate at p = 0.55 drops to p′ ≈ 0.38, and the decoder switches that S1 from "predict it" to "predict nothing". Ye et al. found that decision-theoretic (expected-F) decoding is optimal when the probabilities are right, and that tuned thresholds are more robust when the model is misspecified ([ICML 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4625)). That argues for keeping our expected-F0.5 decoder and adding a few tuned per-slice offsets, rather than replacing the decoder.

**Rank 2: inject test-like decoys into training and validate on folds shifted to look like test.** A decode-time offset cannot repair features that are computed relative to the other candidates of the same S1. Our sibling features, rank-within-S1 features and candidate-count features all change for *true* matches when more decoys land in the same pool. Only training on data that looks like test repairs them. The recipe adapts the "knock-out" and "Dirichlet" shift protocols used to benchmark label-shift estimators ([Lipton et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916)). Inside each GroupKFold training fold, add unmatched near-twin records until the fold's near-twin density per S1 matches test (0.81 → 1.44 in the US, 0.50 → 0.83 in India). Make them by copying real training S2/S3 records of an S1 and perturbing the house number, the same way the training data's own decoys look. For France-like co-location, add different-name records at an S1's exact address. Then rebuild the sibling features, retrain stage 1 and stage 2, and refit the isotonic calibrator on OOF predictions from the injected folds. The same injected folds give us a validation set that looks like test. Report macro F0.5 on them three ways: without correction, with the Rank 1 correction, and with an "oracle" correction that uses the known true prior. That tells us whether each fix closes the gap before we spend a leaderboard submission. The Foursquare 3rd-place author did the equivalent when he held out a test-sized pool, because "retrieval scores depend on the size of the candidate pool" ([Foursquare 3rd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/338112)). Rank 1 and Rank 2 fix the same loss, so their gains overlap and should not be added together. Rank 2 is the durable fix, and it turns CV back into a number that predicts the leaderboard.

**Rank 3: features that compare near-twin groups and measure density or uniqueness.** Inside one S1's candidate pool, near-twin decoys and true matches with house-number noise form competing groups. For example, three records at "12 Main St" and two at "14 Main St", all with the S1's name, where at most one group is the S1's. The cluster-repair literature uses this signal on whole graphs. GraLMatch cuts minimum-edge-cut and high-betweenness "bridge" edges, and in one run lifted a company-group F1 from 0.10 to **99.06** ([De Toni et al., EDBT 2025](https://openproceedings.org/2025/conf/edbt/paper-10.pdf)). TransClean's transitive-consistency check removed **48–97% of false positives** ([arXiv 2506.04006](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04006)). The published comparison of clustering algorithms, on the other hand, says that with groups as small as ours (≤12 records) the choice of clustering algorithm "has no or little effect" ([Draisbach et al. 2019](https://hpi.de/oldsite/fileadmin/user_upload/fachgebiete/naumann/publications/PDFs/2019_draisbach_transforming.pdf)). So the gain lies in *features* fed to stage 2, not in a new clusterer. Examples: the number of distinct house numbers among same-name, same-street candidates; whether this record's house number agrees with the S1's; the size of its house-number group and its score margin over the best rival group. Add density and uniqueness signals as well: how many S1s and how many records share this name, this street, or this exact address. Industry reference-matchers encode exactly this. Dun & Bradstreet's MatchGrade grades Name, Street Number, Street Name and the other fields as A/B/F/Z (same, similar, different, missing) and adds separate **Density and Uniqueness** grades ([D&B Direct 2.0 docs](https://docs.dnb.com/direct/2.0/en-US/company/latest/match/rest-API)). Splink replaces the average chance-agreement probability with a per-value one derived from term frequency, so a match on a rare name earns more weight ([Splink TF docs](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)). Count-based features shift by construction when decoy density rises, so Rank 3 must be trained on Rank 2's injected data. Otherwise it repeats the current failure.

A cheap diagnostic comes before all three ranks. Train a small LightGBM to tell OOF training pairs from test pairs, using *confidently negative pairs only*, and run it per slice. Among shift detectors, classifiers that tell domains apart are the best tool for *characterising* what shifted ([Rabanser et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11953)). The classifier's weights can also reweight the isotonic fit, following the discriminative covariate-shift method of Bickel et al. ([ICML 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1273496.1273507)). If test negatives are easy to tell apart from training negatives in the near-twin slice, the problem is a change in the kind of decoy, and Rank 2 is required rather than optional.

## France needs rules and a probe before it needs adaptation

France is the hardest slice to reason about, because the 0.25 extra "same house number, different name" acceptances per S1 could be decoys (different businesses in the same building) *or* true matches whose names only look different because our normaliser was built for the US and India. The pending leaderboard probe with France rows blanked will show how much France costs. Before that result arrives, one cheap check is worth running. Take a sample of accepted French different-name pairs and see how many differ only by a legal form (SARL, SAS, SASU, EURL, SCI, SELARL), a street-type abbreviation, or accents. La Poste's AFNOR XP Z10-011 tables list **246 street-type abbreviations** (AV, BD, CHEM, IMP, RTE and others) ([La Poste](https://www.laposte.fr/envoyer/abreviation-adresses-postales)), and French legal forms follow the official list ([Service Public](https://entreprendre.service-public.gouv.fr/vosdroits/F23844?lang=en)). A hand-written rule list of about 20 legal forms and the common street types keeps us clearly inside the "no external data" rule. Importing the GLEIF legal-form file (CC0) or libpostal (MIT code, but its normaliser relies on GeoNames/OSM place dictionaries ([Mapzen](https://www.mapzen.com/blog/inside-libpostal/))) would be a judgement call under that rule.

The domain-adaptation literature gives only modest guidance for moving from US+India to France. DADER's feature alignment added +6.8 to +27 F1 between similar entity-matching domains, but only −1.5 to +8.3 when the two datasets already shared vocabulary, and its hyper-parameters were tuned on a *labelled* target validation set that we do not have ([Tu et al., SIGMOD 2022](https://dbgroup.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/ligl/papers/entity-sigmod-2022.pdf)). It also applies to neural encoders, not to LightGBM. Pseudo-labelling unlabelled target pairs was the most important component of Sudowoodo (removing it cost about 10 F1) ([arXiv 2207.04122](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.04122)). It is risky here, because the most confident French pseudo-labels would be drawn from exactly the slice where the model is currently over-accepting. The practical French plan is therefore: rules first; then a per-country offset from Rank 1; then, if the probe shows France is costly, monotone constraints on the name- and address-similarity features, so that trees cannot learn country-specific shortcuts that flip on French data. Monotone constraints have no published entity-matching evidence behind them and would need an ablation that holds out one training country at a time. If we later add a cross-encoder, choose a multilingual one. In cross-language product matching, English-only BERT trained without extra English pairs scored 65.3 F1 on German, against 87.7 for multilingual BERT ([Peeters, Bizer & Glavaš, WWW 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.03338)).

## Beyond the shift, three proven additions raise the ceiling

Once the decoy shift is handled, the gap to 0.985 has to come from better matching. The strongest published template is the 2022 Kaggle Foursquare Location Matching competition, a per-entity-averaged place-matching task structurally close to ours. The 1st-place pipeline went from a LightGBM at CV 0.875 to an mDeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder at 0.907, then a weighted ensemble at 0.911, and finally neighbourhood-merge post-processing at **0.9166** ([re:waiwai write-up](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/336055)). The 2nd-place team fed the transformer's probability into XGBoost as a feature (stacking) ([2nd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/336090)). A **cross-encoder** reads both records as one text and outputs P(match). The earlier Ditto work shows that a plain fine-tuned RoBERTa already accounts for 78.5% of Ditto's average gain ([Ditto, PVLDB 2021](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p50-li.pdf)). Two cautions carry over to our system. First, those +0.03 gains started from a much weaker base than our 0.978. On clean structured benchmarks, similarity-feature models sit within about 1 F1 of transformers, for example DBLP-ACM 98.3 vs 99.0 ([Papadakis et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01231)). Second, transformers are at their worst on exactly our decoys: GPT-4's false positives on Walmart-Amazon were dominated by "ignoring minor but significant differences" such as model numbers ([Peeters et al., EDBT 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11244v4)). So the cross-encoder (Rank 5) should be stacked as a LightGBM feature, trained on the same GroupKFold folds, so that the house-number features keep their veto. It should also score only pairs in the uncertain probability band. It targets our *recall* errors: 40% of missed true matches have empty addresses, and there are DBA aliases, pseudo-word names and transliterations. It does not target the decoy acceptances. Licence-compliant backbones are mDeBERTa-v3-base, XLM-R and mmBERT (all MIT) ([mDeBERTa-v3](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/deberta-v3-base); [mmBERT](https://huggingface.co/jhu-clsp/mmBERT-base)). For throughput, Ditto scored about 7.9M company candidate pairs in 1,339 s on V100 GPUs ([Ditto](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p50-li.pdf)). That puts our ~35M test pairs at a few GPU-hours in full, and under an hour if we score only the uncertain band. This is an extrapolation to the A5000, not a measurement.

Collective assignment is the second addition. It addresses the 20% of misses where another same-name S1 took the record. Microsoft's movie-catalogue study found that global constraints "uniformly" improve on unconstrained matching. It also found that a simple global greedy rule (sort all edges by score, accept an edge when both ends are free) matched or beat exact maximum-weight matching, and got the "Oz" near-twin case right where per-record best choice failed ([Gemmell et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.6016)). A benchmark of 8 such algorithms on 10 datasets recommends Unique Mapping Clustering or the mutual-best rule (EXC) ([Papadakis et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.14030)). Our problem is one S1 to many records, so only the record side is exclusive, and our one-owner step already captures most of the classic gain. What is left is a Rank 6 item: resolve contested records in global score order, and add a "runner-up S1 score and margin" feature so the competition is learned rather than cut hard. Graph post-processing that passes matches through S2–S3 record links was the highest-return late step in Foursquare (+0.0056 for 1st place, +0.022 for a GNN in 13th) ([13th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/336124)). Two differences limit it here. We have a clean hub (S1), and a name-only record's link to another record rests only on its name. Filling a missing field from nearest neighbours was worth +0.009 in Foursquare ([11th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/discussion/335924)), but only because coordinates identified the neighbours, and we have no coordinates.

Blocking is the third addition, and our blocking is already near the published frontier. Sparkly showed that top-k character n-gram BM25 beats every unsupervised dense blocker on all 15 datasets it tested ([Sparkly, PVLDB 2023](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~anhai/papers1/sparkly-vldb2023.pdf)). Only *supervised* contrastive bi-encoders clearly beat it, and on the largest benchmark (WDC-B large, 2.1M records) SC-Block reached 56.7 vs 41.7 recall at k=5 ([SC-Block](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.03132)). Adding the dense retriever's candidates to the lexical ones is what raises recall: combining UniBlocker with Sparkly lifted average recall to 91.7 at a smaller k ([UniBlocker](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.14831)). For India's 2.7% pair-recall loss, a fine-tuned multilingual-e5-small (MIT) or a byte-level name encoder trained on our own Indic↔Latin pairs, with its candidates added to TF-IDF's, is the principled Rank 7. Its F0.5 value is capped, though. At precision 1, raising recall from 0.973 to 0.985 moves F0.5 from about 0.993 to 0.997 for the S1s affected. One licence issue deserves attention now: **anyascii, which our romanisation step uses, is ISC-licensed, not MIT or Apache-2.0** ([anyascii](https://github.com/anyascii/anyascii)). ISC is permissive, but if the rule is read literally, uroman (MIT) is a drop-in candidate ([uroman](https://github.com/isi-nlp/uroman)).

LLMs are last. Open models of 8B parameters or fewer are weak at per-pair matching zero-shot: Llama-3.1-8B averaged 47.75 mean F1 in per-pair mode, against 80.69 for fine-tuned Ditto ([ComEM, COLING 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3)). A fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B reached only 69.19 on WDC Products ([Steiner et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)). Apache-2.0 options exist (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, Mistral-7B), while Jellyfish is CC-BY-NC and Llama 3.x carries Meta's licence, which our rule excludes ([Jellyfish](https://huggingface.co/NECOUDBFM/Jellyfish-8B); [Qwen3-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-8B)). Their one consistent edge is robustness when test data differs from training, which only the France slice would use.

## The ranked plan, with effort, compute and licence

The gains below are our own estimates for this system, reasoned from the measurements and the evidence above. None of them has been measured yet. They overlap, so do not add them up. "Effort" means working days for one person. "CPU" means the 64-core machine; "GPU" means the shared RTX A5000.

| Rank | Technique | What it targets in our measurements | Expected LB gain (estimate) | Effort | Compute | Licence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Per-slice prior correction: EM + BBSE + count matching; logit offset per country × pair type | Extra near-twin and French co-location acceptances | +0.003 to +0.008 | 1 day | CPU, minutes | Own code |
| 2 | Inject test-like decoys into training folds; rebuild sibling features; retrain both stages and isotonic; shifted validation | Same, plus distorted sibling/rank features; makes CV predict LB | +0.003 to +0.010 (overlaps with 1) | 2–3 days | CPU, a few hours per rebuild | LightGBM (MIT) |
| 3 | Group features for near-twins (house-number groups, rival-group margin) + density/uniqueness features | Near-twin decoys, common-name ambiguity | +0.001 to +0.004 | 2 days | CPU, about 1 hour | Own code |
| 4 | French rule normaliser (legal forms, AFNOR street types, accents) + per-country offset; monotone constraints if the probe shows a large France cost | France different-name acceptances | 0 to +0.005 (depends on the probe) | 0.5–1 day | CPU, minutes | Hand-written rules |
| 5 | Stacked multilingual cross-encoder (mDeBERTa-v3-base / XLM-R-base), uncertain band only | Name-only misses (40% of misses), aliases, transliterations | +0.002 to +0.005 | 4–6 days | GPU, about 1–2 days for 5-fold OOF training plus inference | MIT |
| 6 | Global-greedy assignment of contested records + runner-up-S1 margin feature | 20% of misses taken by a same-name S1 | +0.0005 to +0.002 | 1 day | CPU, minutes | Own code |
| 7 | Supervised dense retriever (multilingual-e5-small or byte-level encoder) added to TF-IDF candidates; name-only channel restricted to city/pincode | India pair-recall loss of 2.7% | +0.0005 to +0.002 | 3–4 days | GPU, a few hours; FAISS flat search | MIT / Apache-2.0 |
| 8 | Decoding that models label dependence (a per-S1 true-match-count model feeding the expected-F search) | Several near-identical candidates where at most one is real | ≤ +0.001 | 2–3 days | CPU | Own code |
| 9 | Fine-tuned 7–8B LLM on the uncertain band | France only | Unknown | 5+ days | GPU, days | Qwen (Apache-2.0) only |

Some methods are deliberately left off. Replacing TF-IDF with an unsupervised dense retriever loses to Sparkly-style n-gram search. Swapping in a different clustering algorithm matters little at our group sizes. FAMER, CLIP and GraLMatch as published assume at most one record per source in each cluster, which our S2/S3 violate ([FAMER](https://dbs.uni-leipzig.de/files/research/publications/2018-11/pdf/FAMER-2407-8454-1-SM.pdf)). Domain-adversarial training does not apply to gradient-boosted trees.

## Conclusion

The research changes the order of work more than its content. The industry-standard stack is retrieval from several sources, a filter model, a GBDT with a stacked transformer score, and graph-aware post-processing, and it is mostly already built here. What our measurement adds is that the test set differs from training in a *localised* way, which neither a global prior estimate nor a better per-pair model can detect or fix. The label-shift literature supplies the estimator (per-slice EM with a BBSE cross-check). The Kaggle record supplies the pragmatic anchor (match the predicted set sizes to training). The benchmark literature warns that transformers make look-alike decoys *worse*, not better. Together these point to one plan: fix the decoy shift first, keep the numeric house-number features in charge of decisions, and bring in the cross-encoder afterwards for recall.

The main open risk is the assumption behind Ranks 1 and 2: that the test set added decoys but not true matches in these slices. If the France probe shows that French co-located different-name pairs are mostly genuine matches (for example DBA aliases), then a France offset would cost score, and the effort belongs in French normalisation and a stronger name model instead. Two numbers settle the priority before any retraining: the leaderboard change from the France probe, and the per-slice offsets that best score the injected validation folds. Getting both takes about two days of work and one leaderboard submission.
